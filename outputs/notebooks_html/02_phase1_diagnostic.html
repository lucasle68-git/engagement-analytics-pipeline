Public copy (demo data)¶

The client's 2024 survey cannot be shared publicly, so it is not in this repository. Instead, scripts/make_demo_workbook.py creates data/raw/engagement_survey_2024.xlsx, a stand-in file with the same structure as the real one: the same sheet name, 22 columns, 42 rows, category headers, two 1-10 items and the same delta-from-company-mean format. Only the numbers are invented.

This means the code, the method and the reasoning below are the originals, but every number, table and chart comes from demo data. Phase 1 figures are labelled DEMO instead of REAL. Where a result depends on the client's numbers, the text says what to look for instead of quoting them. Everything the notebooks produce is written to outputs/.

To run on the real data, put the client workbook in data/raw/ and set data.provenance to real in config/config.yaml. The labels then switch back.

What the ranking is read for. Its shape, not its level.

Themes about the immediate working environment (own team, own role, own manager) usually score higher than themes about the more distant layers, heads of department and the executive. This pattern is common in engagement surveys: people rate what they deal with every day more favourably than the layers they rarely meet.

The useful part is elsewhere. The eight themes account for very little of the survey's total variation compared with the spread between departments (section 4 below, and NB03 section D). When the whole ranking fits inside a narrow band, it is a weak basis for action: the distance between the best and the worst theme is smaller than the distance between departments, so a company-wide fix aimed at the lowest theme would miss where the real differences sit.

The next sections therefore move down to department level, and then to item level.

The theme means from this run are in tab03 and fig01.

What this grid is read for: Three patterns, and the third is the one that changes what you would do about it. The examples below come from this demo run (tab04, tab05, fig02).

  1. Clusters of large positive gaps, internal benchmarks, not a league table: Department H sits above the company mean on all eight themes, up to +0.95 on heads of department. The question to ask H is how it does that, not where it ranks.
  2. Two departments, same average, opposite problems: A and E have almost the same average gap (−0.25 and −0.27), but A is evenly below on every theme (all eight between −0.10 and −0.46) while E is pulled down by one cell: line manager at −0.75, with heads of department at −0.02. E has a local problem while A has a departmental climate problem. The two need different responses, and a theme-level average cannot tell them apart.
  3. Large negative gaps at item level inside a high-scoring theme: Department N looks unremarkable on "Working with my Team" (−0.24, inside the company's strongest theme), yet one item in that block, "My team cooperates and helps each other", sits at −0.89. This is the case that matters most: aggregation hides exactly the cells an intervention would target.

3 · What kind of problem is each one?¶

Section 2 read the grid across its rows. Read down its columns, the same grid asks a different question of every item: is a low score a few departments dragging the average down, or every department scoring badly? The two need different interventions.

Decision box
What Score every item on two axes, company mean and spread across the 19 departments and assign it to one of four quadrants.
Why this method A low mean with wide spread is localised (targeted action); a low mean with narrow spread is company-wide (policy fix). Same low score, different response. Both cut-offs are quantiles fixed in config.yaml before any result was seen.
Alternative rejected Flagging on low mean alone, conflates the two problem types and produces one undifferentiated list.
Feeds tab06; fig02b; the item-level targeting used in NB06.

What the quadrant screen establishes: Every item gets two numbers: its company mean, and its spread across departments. The cut-offs come from this survey itself: an item is low if it sits in the bottom quarter on mean (below 3.61), and uneven if it sits in the top quarter on spread (above 0.49) (fig02b). That gives four quadrants, and two readings.

  1. Low score → what kind of low.: An item low in every department is a company-wide problem. An item low in only a few is a local delivery problem. The two need different responses, and an average across departments cannot tell them apart. In this run, 9 of the 34 items fall below the mean cut-off: 4 company-wide (the three executive-team items, plus manageable workload) and 5 localised (work-life balance, speaking up, and three heads-of-department items).

  2. High score, wide spread → uneven strength: This is the fourth quadrant. No ranking of means will show it to you. In this run, 4 items land here. The clearest is "Collaboration between departments/teams", with a mean of 4.20, inside the company's strongest theme. But its scores run from 2.60 to 4.92 across departments. The average calls it a strength; the weakest department is 2.3 points below the strongest. That is why the screen reads spread as well as level.

4 · Eight themes, or one?¶

Decision box
What Correlate the eight theme scores with each other across the 19 department units.
Why this method Shows which themes move togethe (or overlapped), generating hypotheses for the Phase 2 driver work.
Alternative rejected Any inferential reading is prohibited here. ~19 units gives very low power (|r| ≈ 0.46 to clear p < .05), estimates are unstable, and ecological inference does not license individual-level claims.

What the correlation matrix establishes: It compares each theme with every other theme across departments: 28 pairs. Read two things. Neither is a claim about employees: these correlations are between department averages, not between people, so a pattern here says how departments behave as units. Reading it as "employees who feel X also feel Y" is the ecological fallacy (Robinson, 1950). Phase 2 is where that question can be asked properly.

  • Almost everything moves together: In this run all 28 pairs are positive, averaging r = 0.60. A department that rates one theme well tends to rate every theme well. That is a halo, and it is the first of three signs of a general favourability factor. NB03 tests it with PCA and with an independent composite index.

  • The exceptions carry the information: The weakest pair here is own team × executive team, at r = 0.41. Those two move largely on their own, which points to two separate levers rather than one. The strongest pairs both involve "Working for AccessFintech": r = 0.74 with culture, 0.71 with heads of department. That theme holds one of the two 1–10 items. Before reading anything into it, check the scale: the mixed-scale problem from NB01 can inflate a correlation on its own.
