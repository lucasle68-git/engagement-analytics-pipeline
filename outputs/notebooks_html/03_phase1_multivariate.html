Public copy (demo data)¶

The client's 2024 survey cannot be shared publicly, so it is not in this repository. Instead, scripts/make_demo_workbook.py creates data/raw/engagement_survey_2024.xlsx, a stand-in file with the same structure as the real one: the same sheet name, 22 columns, 42 rows, category headers, two 1-10 items and the same delta-from-company-mean format. Only the numbers are invented.

This means the code, the method and the reasoning below are the originals, but every number, table and chart comes from demo data. Phase 1 figures are labelled DEMO instead of REAL. Where a result depends on the client's numbers, the text says what to look for instead of quoting them. Everything the notebooks produce is written to outputs/.

To run on the real data, put the client workbook in data/raw/ and set data.provenance to real in config/config.yaml. The labels then switch back.

Read: two runs, one question each. The level run asks whether departments group by how high they score overall. The shape run centres each department on its own mean first, so it asks whether they group by which themes they are weak in. Only the second would justify the word "archetype".

Judge both against the silhouette convention printed with the table (Kaufman & Rousseeuw, 1990: above 0.50 reasonable structure, 0.25–0.50 weak and possibly artificial, below 0.25 no substantial structure). Then read the membership column next to the score. A run that lifts its silhouette by peeling off one department has found an outlier, not a segment.

In this run nothing clears the bar. The best level score is 0.20 at k = 2, the best shape score is 0.10, and every k beyond 2 starts producing singletons. Both sit below 0.25.

# Robustness check: does a profile structure appear once the outlier is removed?
from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import fcluster, linkage
from scipy.spatial.distance import pdist
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.metrics import silhouette_score

outlier = assign_shape.iloc[:, 0].value_counts().idxmin()
outlier_dept = assign_shape[assign_shape.iloc[:, 0] == outlier].index.to_list()

m = phase1.department_matrix(harmonised).drop(index=outlier_dept)
z = StandardScaler().fit_transform(m.sub(m.mean(axis=1), axis=0))
lnk = linkage(pdist(z, metric="euclidean"), method="ward")

rows = []
for k in range(*[cfg["phase1"]["n_clusters_range"][0], cfg["phase1"]["n_clusters_range"][1] + 1]):
    lab = fcluster(lnk, t=k, criterion="maxclust")
    sizes = pd.Series(lab).value_counts()
    rows.append({"k": k, "silhouette": round(float(silhouette_score(z, lab)), 3),
                 "smallest_cluster": int(sizes.min()), "n_singletons": int((sizes == 1).sum())})

B. Principal component analysis¶

Clustering asked whether departments fall into groups and found they do not. PCA asks a different question: how many dimensions are in the 34 items, and what is each one made of?

Decision box
What PCA on the standardised 19 x 34 matrix, ≤5 components (config), each read from its item loadings.
Why 34 items cannot be inspected raw. PCA compresses them while keeping loadings visible, so each axis can be named and defended.
Boundary p (34) > n (19): components are unstable, so this is structure-revealing, not confirmatory. No rotation, no significance tests neither would be trustworthy here.
Rejected Factor analysis is the right tool, but it needs individual-level data. Deferred to Phase 2, and the gap itself becomes one of the reasons the 2026 survey has to change.

Read: PC1 is not about any one topic. It measures how positively a department answers the survey as a whole.

Two things show this. PC2 sorts by shape: It is built to ignore what PC1 already covers, so it cannot use "high or low" again. It uses the pattern inside a department instead: which items that department rates above its own average, and which below.

To do that, PC2 adds one group of items and subtracts another. Here it adds the progression items (understanding your contribution, career path, recognition) and subtracts the support items (the HR team, sharing ideas, a collaborative culture). A department at +2 rates progression higher than support. One at −2 rates support higher than progression. Either can happen at any level of PC1.

Treat the two labels as a lead, not a finding. PC2 covers 8.5% of the variation, and with only 19 departments a different dataset could put different items at each end. What stays true is the job it does: it tells apart departments that PC1 scores the same. The biplot below shows that.

  • Tilt —> what the biplot catches: Many items each a little off, all leaning the same way. No single cell looks alarming, so a screen that reads cells one at a time will never flag it.

L and K are a tilt case. Both are low overall and almost level on PC1 (−4.5 and −3.9), so the theme averages, the cluster solution and the composite ranking all treat them as the same case. The difference only appears once all 34 items are read together.

The practical difference: a hot spot tells you which cell to fix, a tilt tells you what kind of fix a department needs. Phase 1 needs both, which is why the item screen and the PCA both run.

One limit. Clustering found no groups (section A), so this is a statement about L and K, not a way to sort all 19 departments into two kinds. Coordinates are plotted in fig04b.

C. Importance–Performance: "which weak areas matter?"¶

A low score is worth spending on only if the theme moves with how favourably a department answers overall. This map puts both numbers together (Martilla and James, 1977).

Decision box
What For each theme: how closely it moves with the two anchor items across departments (importance) × its company mean (performance). Both axes split at their medians.
The two anchors "I would recommend AFT to my friends." and "How happy are you at AccessFintech on a scale of 1 to 10?" Set in config/config.yaml under data.outcome_anchors. Both sit inside the theme Working for AccessFintech, which has 6 items in total.
Why these two They ask how people feel overall. The other 32 questions ask about specific conditions: your manager, your workload, your career path. So the anchors are the result, and the rest are possible causes of it. No other question in the survey asks the overall question.
Why this method The most decision-useful technique aggregated data allows: two descriptive numbers become a ranked action list.
Circularity fix The anchors sit inside Working for AccessFintech. Scoring that theme from all 6 of its items and then correlating it with 2 of those same items compares the theme partly with itself, which inflates it. The theme is therefore scored from its 4 non-anchor items only. importance_naive keeps the uncorrected value, so the size of the correction can be checked in tab09.
Rejected Calling these drivers. An association across ~19 units is not a driver model. That is Phase 2.
Feeds tab09, fig04c; the priority interventions in NB06

viz.save_figure(fig, "fig04c_ipma_map", cfg, "real",
                "Importance–Performance map (exploratory, ~19 department units)")

Read: every theme is scored on two things.

Performance: the company average for that theme. How well it scores today.

Importance: how closely the theme tracks a department's overall favourability. Departments that rate this theme highly also answer well across the whole survey: that theme scores high on importance.

The map splits both scores at their median, which gives four boxes. The box that matters is high importance with low performance: themes that move with the overall answer, but score badly today.

Two themes land there in this run: Culture & Wellbeing (importance 0.72, performance 3.81) and Working with the Executive Teams (0.69, 3.07).

Three cautions come with the map.

  1. Importance here is a proxy, not a driver. It is a correlation across 19 department averages, so it shows what moves together, not what causes what. The scores run from 0.53 to 0.85.
  2. Check how far the performance scores spread before trusting a box. Here they run 3.07 to 4.20, wide enough for the split to mean something. If every theme sat within a tenth of a point, the boxes would be arbitrary.
  3. The two anchor items that define importance sit inside one theme, "Working for AccessFintech". Scoring that theme partly against itself pushes it up. The fix scores it from its other items only: 0.94 drops to 0.85. It stays in the same box, so the reading survives its own correction.

One more thing to know. A low importance score usually means the theme does not separate departments, not that it does not matter. Line manager at 0.53 is that case.
